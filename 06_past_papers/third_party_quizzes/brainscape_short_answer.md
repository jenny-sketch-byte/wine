## Question 1 (25 marks)
**Name the 7 different possible wine faults and describe the odours they produce.**

1. **Trichloroanisole (TCA)**: musty odors of damp cardboard.
2. **Reduction**: odor of rotten eggs, boiled cabbage, boiled onions, blocked drains.
3. **Sulfur dioxide**: strong, pungent odor of recently extinguished matches.
4. **Oxidation**: can make the wine smell stale, lacking freshness and fruitiness; may smell
   nutty, or even like burnt marshmallow or stewed fruit; sometimes quite similar to the aromas
   of sherry; wine will look more brown than it should.
5. **Out of condition** – dull, stale aromas. A distinct lack of fruit on the nose.
6. **Volatile acidity** – sharp, pungent aromas of vinegar, nail polish remover.
7. **Brettanomyces** – aromas of "barnyard", fertilizer, plastic, hot leather or vinyl, or sweaty
   farm animals, Band-Aids.

## Question 2 (25 marks)
**Describe the general characteristics of the Riesling grape and discuss how Alsatian Rieslings
differ from Mosel Rieslings.**

Riesling is an aromatic white grape that's late-budding and mid- to late-ripening. This grape
varietal accumulates sugar without losing its naturally high acidity and exhibits tart citrus,
green fruit flavors, and floral notes in cool climates; and ripe citrus and stone fruit character
with richer flavor in warmer climates. As it ages it takes on flavors of honey, toast, and
petrol/diesel/Vasoline. Riesling is one of the world's most long-lived white wines.

Mosel lies at a more northerly latitude than Alsace, and Mosel Rieslings have piercingly high
acidity, usually balanced with perceptible sweetness. They are almost always off-dry, lighter
bodied, and lower in alcohol than Alsace Rieslings. Mosel Rieslings express aromas of lemon,
just-ripe apricot, and blossom. Alsace, on the other hand, exists at a more southerly latitude
than Mosel, and its Rieslings are always dry; they are richer, more textural, more powerful and
weighty than Mosel Rieslings.

Additionally, Alsatian Rieslings are moderate in alcohol (some even flirt with high alcohol at
14%), while Mosel Rieslings are light in alcohol. The fruit profile in Alsatian Rieslings is
concentrated with ripe stone fruits, spice, and bold orchard fruit aromas. Rieslings in Alsace
achieve this ripeness due to the rain shadow caused by the Vosges Mountains.

## Question 3 (25 marks)
**What is a Sauternes, what are the three grape varieties used to make it, and what does each
contribute to the blend? Also detail the flavor characteristics of Botrytis-affected grapes that
go into Sauternes.**

Sauternes is a French sweet wine from the Sauternais region of the Graves section in Bordeaux.
Sauternes is made from Sémillon, Sauvignon Blanc, and Muscadelle grapes that have been affected
by Botrytis cinerea, a fungus also known as noble rot or grey rot. The fungus causes the grapes to
become partially raisinated by puncturing berries with microscopic filaments, leaving tiny holes
in the skin. This results in water evaporating from inside the berry, concentrating acids, sugars,
and flavors.

1. **Sémillon** is a thin-skinned white grape that is particularly susceptible to noble rot. It's
   also capable of aging.
2. **Sauvignon blanc** is an aromatic white grape that adds acidity, herbal notes, and fruitiness.
3. **Muscadelle** adds aromas of exotic perfume.

Botrytis flavor characteristics include honey, ginger spice, dried fruits, saffron, and mushrooms.
This complexity would be impossible to duplicate through normal dehydration, and because Botrytis
infects grapes unevenly, several passes, or tries, must be made through the vineyard to pick
individually affected berries.

## Question 4 (25 marks)
**List and describe the steps taken to produce a traditional method sparkling wine from the point
of creating a base wine.**

**Step 1 — Base wines**: First, the base wines undergo primary fermentation to craft dry,
innocuous, high acid, low-alcohol wines.

**Step 2 — Blending**: Base wines are blended with close attention to consistency — especially
for NV sparkling wines — as well as complexity and balance. Different grapes, vineyards,
vintages, and press fractions are utilized to create a layering of flavors. Blending is important
to achieve a house style, improve balance, and enhance depth and complexity.

**Step 3 — Second fermentation**: The blended wine then undergoes second fermentation after
liqueur de tirage is added (a mixture of yeast, sugar, nutrients, base wine, and clarifying
agent). This should be a slow and cool fermentation in order to develop pleasant flavor
characteristics and to create a fine mousse. This second fermentation happens inside a bottle
(rather than a tank) which has been enclosed with a crown cap and bidule.

**Step 4 — Yeast autolysis**: Longer, slower, and cooler conditions on the lees generates small,
fine, and persistent bubbles. Also, the longer the aging sur lie, the more autolytic development
will take place, which creates flavors of brioche, toast, bread, and biscuit.

**Step 5 — Riddling**: The aim of riddling is to cajole all lees and sediment produced from
second fermentation into the neck of the bottle (into the bidule) where it will be disgorged.
This can be done by hand (8 weeks) or gyropalette (3–4 days).

**Step 6 — Disgorgement**: The neck of the bottle is frozen in a liquid brine and the crown cap
removed. The pressure of the CO2 from inside the bottle forces the sediment/yeast plug out of the
neck, rendering the wine clear. A quick addition of dosage, also known as liqueur d'expédition
(wine and sugar), is added to determine the final sweetness/style of the wine. This is the
winemaker's final opportunity to achieve balance before the wine bottle is sealed.

**Step 7 — Corking**: Immediately after dosage the wine is enclosed with a mushroom cork and a
muselet (wire cage) is placed on top of and around the neck to secure the cork.

**Step 8 — Bottle Aging**: At least several months of aging for all traditional method sparkling
wines to integrate the final dosage; Vintage and other premium sparkling wines will see extended
bottle aging. Bottle aging allows the wine to continue to develop flavors, including those of
mushrooms and caramel. Most Vintage Blanc de Blancs will benefit from bottle age as they are
severe and angular in their youth.
