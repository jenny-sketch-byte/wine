# "How to Answer WSET Level 3 Written Questions" — Video Training Script

A recordable script for a training video teaching WSET Level 3 written-answer technique:
reading command words correctly and understanding how marks are actually awarded.
Companion tool: the **Field Sheets** site (`web/index.html`) — specifically Written Practice.

Target length: ~13 minutes. Format: voiceover over screen recording — no on-camera talent
required. Every line below is written to be read aloud as-is; `[VISUAL: ...]` cues tell
whoever is recording what to show on screen at that moment.

**Audience note:** non-native English speakers sitting an English-language exam. Speak
slightly slower than natural pace, pause after key terms, avoid idioms and contractions
in the narration (already applied in the script below). Don't add Hebrew narration — the
real exam is in English, so this video should model the English they'll actually need. If
you want on-screen support, add short Hebrew text captions under key terms (mirroring the
bilingual style already used on the Field Sheets site) rather than dubbing.

**Attribution note:** the central technique in Segment 3 ("keep asking so what until you
reach the glass") is adapted from a teaching device Napa Valley Wine Academy calls the
"Golden Thread" ([source](https://napavalleywineacademy.com/blogs/pouring-points/wset-level-3-study-guide-how-to-master-the-why-and-how)).
Credit them by name in the video description if you publish this — the technique itself
is a widely taught idea, but the name and framing are theirs.

## Segment 1 — Cold open (0:00–0:45)

`[VISUAL: Black screen, then white text fades in, centered, one line at a time]`

> Here is a sentence that gets zero marks on a WSET Level 3 exam.
>
> "Alsace has a cool climate protected by mountains."
>
> That sentence is completely true. It is also worth nothing, if the question asked you
> to explain.
>
> In the next few minutes, I am going to show you exactly why — and exactly what to write
> instead.

`[VISUAL: Cut to title card: "How to Answer WSET Level 3 Written Questions"]`

---

## Segment 2 — Why Level 3 is different (0:45–2:00)

`[VISUAL: Left side of screen shows a multiple-choice question with four bubbles; right side shows a blank lined answer space]`

> WSET Level 2 tests what you know. You recognize the right fact among four options, and
> you are done.
>
> WSET Level 3 tests something different. It tests whether you can connect what you know
> to a result. The written section is not asking you to recall facts anymore. It is asking
> you to reason with them.
>
> This is exactly where most candidates lose marks — not because they do not know the
> content, but because they answer a Level 3 question with a Level 2 mindset. They write
> down a correct fact, and they stop.
>
> A correct fact, on its own, is usually worth half marks or less. The rest of the marks
> live in what comes after the fact.

`[VISUAL: Text overlay: "A fact is not an answer. A fact is the START of an answer."]`

---

## Segment 3 — The six command words (2:00–5:00)

`[VISUAL: Screen recording — open the Field Sheets Written Practice page, click open the "Command words" glossary panel]`

> Before you write a single word in the exam, you do one thing: you find the command word
> in the question, and you underline it. This single habit prevents more lost marks than
> anything else I can teach you.
>
> There are six command words you need to know cold.

`[VISUAL: For each command word, show it large on screen with the WSET-style example beneath it]`

> Number one: **Identify**, **State**, or **Name**. This wants a fact — a term, a place, a
> grape. Nothing more. If you write a full sentence of explanation here, you are not
> earning extra marks. You are wasting time you need later.
>
> Number two: **Describe**. This wants characteristics — what something IS, or what it
> looks like. Not why. If I ask you to describe Chablis soil, the answer is: "Chablis has
> Kimmeridgian limestone and clay soil with marine fossils." That is a complete, full-marks
> answer to a Describe question. No cause and effect required.
>
> Number three: **Explain**. This is the one that matters most, because most of your marks
> live here. Explain wants why, or how. It wants a cause, leading to a mechanism, leading
> to an effect. If your answer does not contain the word "because," or the phrase "this
> means that," somewhere in it — you have probably only described. You have not explained.
>
> Number four: **Outline**. A short summary of several points. Go for breadth, not depth.
> One or two sentences per point is enough.
>
> Number five: **Compare**. This wants similarities AND differences between two things. If
> you only write about one side, or you never state what the two things have in common,
> you lose marks — even if everything you wrote is correct.
>
> Number six: **Discuss**. This wants multiple angles or factors, weighed against each
> other. It is the broadest command word, and it usually appears in longer-answer
> questions.

`[VISUAL: Show all six side by side one more time, then zoom into "Explain" and "Compare" — highlight in a different color]`

> If you remember only two of these, remember Explain and Compare. They are the two most
> commonly misread, and they are the two most heavily weighted in marks.

---

## Segment 4 — The Golden Thread (5:00–8:30)

`[VISUAL: Whiteboard-style — white background, black marker-style text appears as if being written]`

> So how do you actually write an Explain answer that gets full marks? There is one
> technique that will change how you write more than anything else. I call it the Golden
> Thread.
>
> Here is the rule: every time you state a fact, do not stop. Ask yourself one question —
> "so what?" — and keep asking it, until your answer reaches the glass. Until it reaches
> the actual wine, in someone's hand.

`[VISUAL: Build this chain on screen, one arrow at a time, each with a pause]`

> Watch what happens when you apply this to a real example.
>
> Start with a fact: "Alsace has a cool climate, protected by the Vosges Mountains."
>
> So what? "This creates a rain shadow — the mountains block Atlantic weather systems."
>
> So what? "This means Alsace has low rainfall and reduced disease pressure."
>
> So what? "This means grapes can safely hang on the vine well past normal ripeness,
> without the risk of rot."
>
> So what? "This means producers can pick at different points — from dry wine, to late
> harvest, to fully botrytized sweet wine — all from the same grape variety."
>
> That is five steps. That is five sentences. And notice something: only the first
> sentence is a fact you memorized. Every sentence after that is you reasoning forward,
> using the word "means," or "this results in," or "this allows."

`[VISUAL: Text overlay: "One fact. Four consequences. That is a full-marks Explain answer."]`

> This is the difference between a student who has memorized the textbook, and a student
> who understands the subject. The examiner cannot see inside your head. The Golden Thread
> is how you make your reasoning visible, on paper, so the examiner can award marks for it.
>
> One more thing about facts without consequences: in the actual mark scheme, a fact
> sitting on its own is usually worth about half the marks of that same fact connected to
> an outcome. If you mention "cool climate," and you stop there, you have earned partial
> credit at best. The moment you connect it to "high acidity," or "slow, even ripening,"
> you unlock the rest of the marks available for that point.

## Segment 5 — Worked example: Explain (8:30–12:00)

`[VISUAL: Screen recording — Field Sheets, Written Practice, filtered to "Regions," landed on the Bordeaux Explain question]`

> Let's do this for real, using a genuine WSET-style question. Here it is:
>
> "Explain why Cabernet Sauvignon dominates the Left Bank of Bordeaux, while Merlot
> dominates the Right Bank."

`[VISUAL: Show a weak answer typed out on screen, text appearing as if being written]`

> Here is a weak answer. Watch for where it stops.
>
> "The Left Bank has gravel soil. The Right Bank has clay soil. Cabernet Sauvignon grows on
> the Left Bank and Merlot grows on the Right Bank."
>
> This answer is completely factually correct. And it would score close to zero, on an
> Explain question. Why? Because it is four separate facts, sitting next to each other,
> with no thread connecting them. There is no "because." There is no mechanism. It reads
> like a Describe answer, not an Explain answer.

`[VISUAL: Clear the screen. Build the strong answer using the Golden Thread, one arrow at a time]`

> Now watch the same facts, run through the Golden Thread.
>
> Fact: "The Left Bank has well-drained gravel soil that retains daytime heat."
>
> So what? "This suits Cabernet Sauvignon, which ripens late and needs a long, warm season
> to fully ripen its thick skins."
>
> Now the other side — because this question also has the word Compare hiding inside it,
> even though the command word is Explain. You must address both banks.
>
> Fact: "The Right Bank has cooler, water-retentive clay and limestone soil."
>
> So what? "This suits Merlot, which ripens earlier and is more sensitive to drought
> stress, so it benefits from a soil that holds moisture."
>
> Now close the loop — this is the sentence that pulls the whole answer together and shows
> the examiner you understood the actual question, not just two separate facts:
>
> "This means the two banks favor different grapes, based on how well each variety's
> ripening needs match the soil's drainage and heat-retention properties."

`[VISUAL: Show both answers side by side one final time — weak on the left, strong on the right, with a large checkmark on the strong one]`

> Same knowledge. Same facts. The only difference is the thread connecting them. That
> thread is worth more marks than any extra fact you could add.

---

## Segment 6 — Worked example: Compare (12:00–13:30)

`[VISUAL: Field Sheets, Written Practice, filtered to "Foundations," Viticulture, Compare question]`

> One more, because Compare deserves its own example — it fails in a different way than
> Explain does.
>
> "Compare hand harvesting and machine harvesting."

`[VISUAL: Show a one-sided answer]`

> Here is the trap. A student who only talks about hand harvesting — even in perfect,
> detailed, well-connected sentences — will still lose marks on a Compare question. Why?
> Because Compare has two requirements, not one: similarities, AND differences. If you only
> cover one method, you have not compared anything. You have described one thing.

`[VISUAL: Build a two-column answer, one column per method, then a final sentence linking them]`

> The fix is structural, not just about content. Give each side its own space. Hand
> harvesting: selective, can leave unripe or diseased fruit behind, essential on steep
> slopes, but expensive. Machine harvesting: fast, cheap, can pick at night to keep fruit
> cool, but not selective, and only works on flat, accessible land.
>
> Then — and this is the step people skip — say what connects them: "The choice reflects a
> trade-off between cost and selectivity." One sentence. It proves you are comparing, not
> just listing two facts next to each other.

---

## Segment 7 — How to practice this (13:30–14:30)

`[VISUAL: Field Sheets sidebar, hover over "Practice" group, click into Written Practice]`

> You cannot learn this by reading about it. You learn it by doing it, badly, on purpose,
> and then checking your own work.
>
> That is exactly what Written Practice is built for. Pick a question. Set a timer if you
> want real exam pressure. Write your answer — do not look at the model answer first. Then
> reveal the model answer, and go through the checklist one item at a time: did you name
> the factor? Did you state the mechanism? Did you reach an outcome? Did you address both
> sides, if the question asked you to compare?
>
> Be honest about where you lost marks — content, the command word, vocabulary, or
> structure — and save it. Do that ten times, and you will start writing Golden Thread
> answers automatically, without thinking about the technique at all.

---

## Segment 8 — Recap and close (14:30–15:00)

`[VISUAL: Text summary card, one line fades in at a time]`

> Three things to remember.
>
> One: find the command word first. Underline it before you write anything.
>
> Two: a fact is the start of an answer, not the end of one. Keep asking "so what," until
> you reach the glass.
>
> Three: for Compare and Discuss, both sides need real space on the page — one-sided
> answers lose marks, no matter how detailed.
>
> Go practice. The questions are waiting for you.

`[VISUAL: End card — "Field Sheets" logo mark, link/QR code to the site]`
