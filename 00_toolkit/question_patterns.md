# Question Patterns — What WSET Level 3 Actually Asks

Built directly from the WSET Level 3 Sample Short Written Answer Paper (Oct 2017),
4 questions × 25 marks = 100 marks total. See `06_past_papers/` for the source. This is
not a guess at question style — these are the real archetypes, with real mark weights,
that the sample paper uses.

## The core discipline: marks are per discrete point, not per paragraph

A question worth 12 marks is not "write a lot." It is usually several small, separately-
markable points added together. Question 3b in the sample paper — *"identify TWO natural
factors and explain how they moderate the climate"* for two regions — is worth 12 marks
because it is really **four mini-answers**: factor + mechanism, twice, for two regions.
Roughly 1 mark for correctly identifying the factor, 2 marks for correctly explaining the
mechanism — repeated four times.

**What this means for how you write:** structure your answer in labeled chunks that
mirror the question's own structure — one clearly separated chunk per sub-part, per
factor, per region. A marker scanning your answer for discrete points cannot award marks
for reasoning buried inside one long paragraph. If the question gives you blank
lines/headings, that is a map of exactly where each mark lives — use it.

## The archetypes

| Pattern | Real example from the sample paper | Marks | Answer shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Name + Describe (multiple)** | "Name and describe TWO methods of production used for making rosé wines in the Southern Rhône." | 7 | One named method + 1-2 sentence description, repeated per method. The name alone earns little; the description is where most marks sit. |
| **Direct technical question (no command word)** | "What are the main considerations when selecting grapes for semi-carbonic maceration?" | 2 | Short, factual, no preamble needed — treat like Identify/State despite the phrasing. |
| **Describe the process** | "Describe the process of semi-carbonic maceration." | 4 | Sequential steps, in order — characteristics/mechanics of the process, not why it's chosen. |
| **Structured style-prediction (SAT-style headings)** | "Under the headings below describe the typical style of a newly released, inexpensive red Côtes du Rhône..." (Nose / Palate: Sweetness, Tannin, Body / Conclusions) | 6 | One short answer per heading. This is a compressed SAT (Systematic Approach to Tasting) — see `00_toolkit/vocab.md`. Skipping a heading loses those marks outright, even if your other headings are excellent. |
| **Stylistic + Practical split** | "State the stylistic and practical reasons why a winemaker in this region might choose to blend." | 6 | Two explicitly separate categories. A blended, unlabeled list of reasons loses marks even if the content is correct — the marker is checking for reasons in *both* buckets. |
| **Label reverse-engineering** | "Under the following headings describe the two wines pictured above" (given only label images) | 10 | No wine description is given — you predict sweetness/body/aroma/quality entirely from label evidence (region, appellation tier, vintage descriptor, producer). See `00_toolkit/reverse_engineering_framework.md` for the method. |
| **Describe + Explain combined** | "Describe the key natural factors in Alsace and explain how these contribute to the style of Wine B." | 7 | Two-part answer in sequence: first the facts (Describe), then the causal chain from those facts to the wine's style (Explain). Doing only one half loses roughly half the marks. |
| **Pure Explain (why)** | "Large, old oak barrels used in Alsace have little influence on the wines' aromas. Explain why." | 3 | One clean cause → mechanism → effect chain. Short mark value means one solid chain, not several. |
| **Identify + Explain, per factor, per place** | "For each region, identify TWO natural factors and explain how they moderate the climate." (Los Carneros / Central Otago) | 12 | Four repeated mini-units: name the factor, then explain its mechanism. See the worked example in `reverse_engineering_framework.md`. |
| **Identify + Explain, per domain** | "Identify ONE human influence in the vineyard and ONE human influence in the winery... Explain how they do so." | 6 | Same pattern as above, but split across two different domains (vineyard vs. winery) instead of two places. |
| **Explain a producer's choice** | "Explain why the producer of an outstanding quality Chardonnay... might choose to use malolactic fermentation." | 5 | Reasoning from a stated goal (outstanding quality, a specific style) backward to a technique — not just "what MLF does" but why *this producer, this wine* would choose it. |
| **Practical / service knowledge** | "What storage conditions would you recommend for the long-term ageing of this wine?" / "Describe the process of opening and decanting a fully mature Vintage Port." | 5 | Procedural, not climate/soil reasoning. A real content category with its own knowledge base — flagged as a gap in `reverse_engineering_framework.md`. |

## Reading a question before you write

1. Count the sub-parts (a, b, c...) and their mark values first. A 2-mark part wants a
   short, direct answer. A 10-mark part wants a fully developed, multi-point answer —
   writing three sentences for 10 marks is leaving marks on the table as surely as writing
   three paragraphs for 2 marks is wasting time.
2. Check for headings, blank-line structures, or "under the following headings" language.
   That structure is not decoration — it is the marker's checklist, visible to you in
   advance.
3. Check for a doubled command word ("Name and describe," "Identify... and explain") —
   these are two separate mark pools disguised as one sentence. Answer both halves,
   explicitly.
4. Check for a split category word ("stylistic AND practical," "vineyard AND winery") —
   these want two visibly separate answers, not one merged list.

## Self-check

Before submitting a practice answer, count how many separately-markable points you can
find in your own writing, and compare that count to the mark value of the question. If a
7-mark question only has two identifiable points in your answer, you have likely under-
developed it — even if every word you wrote is correct.
