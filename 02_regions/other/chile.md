# Chile

## Climate
- Type: **Mediterranean** overall — warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters. Very
  reliable, with low vintage variation compared with maritime Europe.
- The exam-critical point is that Chile is a *warm* country that nonetheless
  produces genuinely cool-climate wines, and you must be able to explain the two
  mechanisms that make that possible.
- **Cooling influence 1 — the Humboldt Current.** A cold ocean current runs north
  up the Chilean coast from Antarctic waters. It chills the air above it, and that
  cold air is drawn inland as sea breezes and as morning fog (the *camanchaca*).
  Sites exposed to it ripen slowly and retain high acidity.
- **Cooling influence 2 — the Andes.** At night, cold air drains down off the
  mountains onto the vineyards below. This produces a large diurnal range: warm
  days build sugar and flavour, cold nights preserve acidity and aroma.
- **The Coastal Range is the gatekeeper.** This low mountain range runs between
  the ocean and the Central Valley. Where it is continuous, it blocks the maritime
  influence and the land behind it is warm and dry. Where it is broken by gaps and
  river valleys, cold air and fog funnel through to the vineyards inland. This
  single fact explains why Casablanca and San Antonio are cool while Maipo, at a
  similar latitude, is warm.
- Hazards: **drought** is the dominant one, with irrigation near-universal;
  also spring frost in some valleys, and occasional El Niño rainfall at harvest.

## Phylloxera
Chile has never suffered a phylloxera epidemic, so a large proportion of vines are
**ungrafted, planted on their own roots**. The reasons are its natural isolation —
the Atacama Desert to the north, the Andes to the east, the Pacific to the west and
Antarctic waters to the south — combined with sandy soils that the louse struggles
to move through. This is a favourite MCQ fact.

## Soils
- Highly varied. Alluvial gravel and sand on the valley floors, granite and
  volcanic material in the Coastal Range, and clay in places.
- **Limarí is the notable one**: unusually for Chile it has limestone, which
  together with its cool coastal position is why it has a reputation for
  Chardonnay.

## Key grapes
- **Carmenère** — the signature variety, and the one most likely to be examined.
  Originally a Bordeaux grape, it was widely mistaken for Merlot in Chilean
  vineyards until DNA testing identified it in 1994. It **ripens very late** and
  demands a warm site with a long season. If picked underripe it shows aggressive
  green, herbaceous, capsicum-like pyrazine character; fully ripe it gives soft
  tannins and dark, spiced fruit. Warm sites in Colchagua and Cachapoal suit it.
- **Cabernet Sauvignon** — the most planted red and the backbone of premium Chile,
  especially in Maipo and its Andean foothill sub-zone Alto Maipo.
- **Merlot**, **Syrah** (Elqui, coastal sites), **Pinot Noir** (cool coastal only).
- **Sauvignon Blanc** — Casablanca, San Antonio/Leyda; **Chardonnay** — Limarí,
  Casablanca.
- **País** and old-vine **Carignan** in the south (Maule), historically bulk
  varieties now attracting quality interest.

## Regions (as named in the Specification)
- **Coquimbo** — the far north, hot and desert-influenced, saved by altitude and
  coastal exposure. *Elqui* (high altitude, Syrah), *Limarí* (cool, coastal,
  limestone, Chardonnay).
- **Aconcagua** — contains Chile's coolest sites. *Casablanca* and *San Antonio*
  (with its sub-zone *Leyda*) sit in Coastal Range gaps and are fog-cooled:
  Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Pinot Noir. *Aconcagua Valley* itself is warm, with
  cooler coastal and higher Andean sectors.
- **Central Valley** — the volume heartland, warm and irrigated. *Maipo*
  (Cabernet), *Cachapoal* and *Colchagua* (together the Rapel Valley; Carmenère,
  Syrah, Cabernet), *Curicó* and *Maule* (high volume; Maule also has old-vine
  Carignan and País).
- **Southern Region** — cooler and wetter, further from the equator.

## Law
Chile uses a **DO** system based on region and sub-region. Alongside it sits a set
of designations introduced in 2011 that describe a vineyard's **east–west** position
rather than its north–south one, which is the more meaningful axis in a country
where the cooling comes from the coast and the mountains:
- **Costa** — coastal, cooled by the Humboldt Current
- **Entre Cordilleras** — between the two mountain ranges, warmest
- **Andes** — the Andean foothills, cooled by altitude and night-time air drainage

## Quality/price factors
Entry-level Chile comes from high-yielding, irrigated, machine-harvested valley
floor sites in Curicó and Maule, and competes on price. Premium Chile comes from
identified cool coastal sites or Andean foothill slopes, with lower yields, hand
harvesting and often ungrafted old vines. The story of the last two decades has
been the deliberate move away from the warm middle of the Central Valley towards
these cooler edges.

## Likely exam angles

**"Explain how a warm, Mediterranean country like Chile is able to produce
cool-climate wine styles."** This is the central Chilean question, and it needs the
two cooling mechanisms plus the role of the Coastal Range.

> "Although Chile has a warm Mediterranean climate, two factors create genuinely
> cool sites. The cold Humboldt Current chills the air above the Pacific, and this
> cold air and fog is drawn inland wherever there are gaps in the Coastal Range,
> as at Casablanca and San Antonio, slowing ripening and retaining high acidity.
> Separately, cold air drains down from the Andes at night, creating a wide
> diurnal range that preserves acidity and aroma in vineyards on the eastern
> foothills. This means producers can make fresh, aromatic wines such as Sauvignon
> Blanc and Pinot Noir despite the country's overall warmth."

**"Explain why Carmenère is planted in warm sites in Chile."**

> "Carmenère is a very late-ripening variety, so it requires a warm site with a
> long growing season to reach full ripeness. If it is harvested underripe it
> retains high levels of pyrazines, giving aggressive green, herbaceous flavours
> that reduce quality. Warm inland zones such as Colchagua and Cachapoal supply
> the necessary heat, allowing the grape to ripen fully and produce soft tannins
> and ripe dark fruit."
