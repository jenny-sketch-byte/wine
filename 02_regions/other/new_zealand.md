# New Zealand (Marlborough, Hawke's Bay, Martinborough, Central Otago)

## Climate
- Type: **Cool maritime** throughout — no part of New Zealand is far from the
  ocean, which moderates temperature everywhere and is the starting point for
  almost every regional story here.
- High **sunshine hours** relative to the cool temperatures, thanks to a
  relatively clean, unpolluted atmosphere and high UV levels at this southern
  latitude: grapes get strong light for photosynthesis without excessive heat.
- Hazards: **spring frost**, and **rain around harvest** given the maritime
  pattern, which is the main source of vintage variation.
- **Central Otago** is the deliberate exception: inland and semi-continental
  rather than maritime, which is precisely what makes it distinct (see
  `00_toolkit/reverse_engineering_framework.md` for the full worked comparison
  with Los Carneros).

## Marlborough
- The dominant region by volume, at the north end of the South Island.
- **Wairau Valley**: the original, larger zone. Free-draining, stony
  river-deposited soils over a silty topsoil; relatively warmer and more
  fertile within the Marlborough context.
- **Awatere Valley**: further south, cooler and windier, on clay-based soils;
  tends to give a leaner, more herbaceous, more mineral style than Wairau
  fruit.
- **Sauvignon Blanc** dominates: New Zealand's single most planted variety,
  and Marlborough is its heartland. The cool climate preserves high acidity
  and drives strong thiol expression (compounds behind the passionfruit,
  gooseberry and blackcurrant-leaf/capsicum aromatics), and winemaking choices
  reinforce this: cool, temperature-controlled fermentation and yeast strains
  selected specifically to maximise thiol release. This combination of
  climate and winemaking technique is what produces the pungent, unmistakable
  style that defines the region internationally.
- Also grown: Pinot Noir (a meaningful secondary crop, some for still wine,
  some as sparkling base), Pinot Gris, Chardonnay.

## Hawke's Bay
- North Island, warmer and drier than the South Island regions, with a longer
  history of viticulture than Marlborough.
- **Gimblett Gravels**: a free-draining, low-fertility, heat-retaining
  shingle soil deposited by a former riverbed. It is meaningfully warmer than
  surrounding land, which allows it to ripen Bordeaux varieties and Syrah
  reliably in a country whose reputation is otherwise built on cool-climate
  whites and Pinot Noir.
- Key grapes: **Cabernet Sauvignon**, **Merlot** and other Bordeaux varieties
  (often blended), **Syrah**, plus **Chardonnay**.

## Martinborough (Wairarapa)
- Southern tip of the North Island. Cool and windy, with low, free-draining,
  alluvial soils.
- Small in volume but disproportionately reputed for **Pinot Noir**, made in
  a generally more structured, savoury style than much South Island Pinot.

## Central Otago
- The world's southernmost wine region and New Zealand's only significant
  region with a **semi-continental**, inland climate rather than a maritime
  one, at real altitude for New Zealand.
- Almost synonymous with **Pinot Noir**, made in a riper, more concentrated
  style than Martinborough, reflecting the wider diurnal range and sunnier,
  more settled summers that its altitude and inland position provide.

## Other regions named in the Specification
- **Nelson**: adjacent to Marlborough, smaller and more maritime-influenced;
  similar grape mix on a smaller scale.
- **Gisborne**: North Island, warm and maritime; historically known for bulk
  production, now increasingly associated with quality **Chardonnay**.
- **Canterbury**: South Island, cool, includes the Waipara Valley sub-region;
  Pinot Noir and Riesling.

## Winemaking note: screwcap closures
New Zealand was an early and near-universal adopter of the **screwcap**
closure, including for premium wine. The reasoning is consistency-driven:
screwcaps eliminate cork taint (TCA) and give reliable, predictable oxygen
ingress, which matters most for preserving the fresh, aromatic, high-acid
style that defines the country's flagship wines, above all Marlborough
Sauvignon Blanc.

## Law: Geographical Indications (GI)
New Zealand uses a **Geographical Indications** system that defines origin
only. Unlike AOC or DOC, it sets **no rules on permitted grape varieties,
yields, or winemaking methods** — the same origin-only structure seen in the
US AVA system and South Africa's Wine of Origin scheme, and a useful point of
direct comparison if a question asks you to contrast New World and Old World
labelling law.

## Quality/price factors
Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc's distinctive, unmistakable style commands a
consistent international price premium relative to Sauvignon Blanc from
elsewhere, driven by climate and winemaking technique rather than by an
appellation hierarchy. Pinot Noir from Martinborough and Central Otago
occupies New Zealand's premium red tier, reflecting low yields and the
international reputation both regions have built for the variety.

## Likely exam angles

**"Explain how climate and winemaking together produce the characteristic
style of Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc."**

> "Marlborough's cool maritime climate, combined with high sunshine hours,
> allows Sauvignon Blanc to ripen slowly while retaining high acidity and
> developing strong thiol compounds, which are responsible for the variety's
> passionfruit, gooseberry and capsicum aromatics. Winemakers reinforce this
> naturally aromatic profile with cool, temperature-controlled fermentation
> and yeast strains chosen specifically to maximise thiol expression,
> producing the pungent, instantly recognisable style the region is known
> for internationally."

**"Explain why Gimblett Gravels in Hawke's Bay is suited to Bordeaux
varieties, unlike much of the rest of New Zealand."**

> "The Gimblett Gravels soil is a free-draining, low-fertility shingle
> deposited by a former riverbed, which retains daytime heat and radiates it
> back to the vines. Combined with Hawke's Bay's position as one of New
> Zealand's warmer regions, this gives Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and other
> Bordeaux varieties the sustained warmth they need to ripen fully, which
> most of New Zealand's cooler, wetter maritime sites cannot reliably
> provide."
