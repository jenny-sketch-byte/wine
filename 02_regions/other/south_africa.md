# South Africa

## Climate
- Type: **Mediterranean** — warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters, at the
  southern tip of the African continent.
- As with Chile, the examinable question is how a warm country produces fresh,
  cool-climate styles. South Africa has three answers.
- **The Benguela Current.** A cold current flows north up the west coast from
  Antarctic waters. It cools the air above it, and coastal and south-facing sites
  exposed to that air ripen more slowly and hold acidity.
- **The Cape Doctor.** A strong, persistent south-easterly wind blows through the
  Cape in summer. It has a genuine double effect and both halves earn marks: it
  **cools** the vineyards and **dries the canopy, reducing fungal disease
  pressure**, which is a real benefit given the wet winters; but it can also
  **damage shoots and disrupt flowering**, reducing yields, and vines may need
  training systems that offer shelter.
- **Altitude and aspect.** Vineyards on the granite foothills of the Cape
  mountains, and those planted on cooler south-facing slopes, are meaningfully
  cooler than the valley floors.
- Hazards: **drought** (severe in recent years, with irrigation restricted in
  places), wind damage, and historically **leafroll virus**, which delays ripening
  and reduces quality until infected vineyards are replanted.

## Soils
- **Granite** on the mountain foothills of Stellenbosch and Paarl — good water
  retention, which matters in a dry-farmed, drought-prone country.
- **Sandstone** and **shale** elsewhere; Swartland is known for decomposed granite
  and shale supporting unirrigated bush vines.
- Table Mountain sandstone is generally poor and free-draining.

## Key grapes
- **Chenin Blanc** — the most planted variety in the country, locally called
  **Steen**. Historically used for bulk and brandy production, it is now the
  source of some of South Africa's best whites, particularly from old, dry-farmed
  bush vines in Swartland. Styles run from simple and fruity to concentrated,
  oak-matured and ageworthy.
- **Pinotage** — a **crossing of Pinot Noir and Cinsault**, created in South Africa
  in 1925 by Abraham Perold. It is the country's own variety and a likely MCQ
  target. It ripens early and accumulates sugar readily, giving high alcohol. It
  is made in two broad styles: a lighter, fruity style, and a rich, heavily oaked
  style, sometimes marketed with pronounced sweet-toast "coffee" character.
- **Cabernet Sauvignon**, **Syrah/Shiraz**, **Merlot** — the main premium reds,
  often blended in a Bordeaux style.
- **Sauvignon Blanc** — cool coastal sites (Elim, Elgin, Constantia, Durbanville).
- **Chardonnay** and **Pinot Noir** — Hemel-en-Aarde and Elgin.

## Regions (as named in the Specification)
- **Western Cape** — the overarching Geographical Unit covering essentially all
  quality production.
- **Coastal Region** — *Stellenbosch* (the premium heartland; granite foothills,
  Cabernet and Bordeaux blends), *Paarl* (warmer, inland, Shiraz and Chenin),
  *Constantia* (historic, cool and maritime, Sauvignon Blanc, and the home of the
  famous sweet Muscat wine), *Durbanville* (cool, maritime, Sauvignon Blanc),
  *Swartland* (warm and dry, unirrigated old bush vines, Rhône varieties and
  premium Chenin, and the centre of the country's low-intervention movement).
- **Breede River Valley** — *Worcester* and *Robertson*. Hot and dry, irrigated
  from the Breede River, historically high-volume; Robertson has limestone and a
  reputation for Chardonnay.
- **Cape South Coast** — the cool end of the country. *Walker Bay* and its ward
  *Hemel-en-Aarde* (Pinot Noir and Chardonnay), *Elim* (very cool and windy, close
  to the southern tip), *Elgin* (high-altitude plateau, cool, Sauvignon Blanc and
  Chardonnay).

## Law: the Wine of Origin (WO) scheme
A certification of **origin only** — it does not dictate permitted grape varieties,
yields or winemaking methods, which distinguishes it from the French AOC model.
The hierarchy runs from broad to narrow:

**Geographical Unit** (e.g. Western Cape) → **Region** (e.g. Coastal Region) →
**District** (e.g. Stellenbosch) → **Ward** (e.g. Hemel-en-Aarde)

A wine labelled with a WO area must come **100%** from it. **Estate Wine** is a
separate designation for wine grown, made and bottled on a single registered
property. Where a vintage or variety is stated, minimum percentage rules apply.

## Quality/price factors
The Breede River Valley supplies irrigated, high-yielding fruit for inexpensive,
high-volume brands. Premium wine comes from Stellenbosch's granite slopes, the cool
Cape South Coast, and Swartland's low-yielding old dry-farmed bush vines — the last
of these commanding high prices precisely because unirrigated old vines produce
tiny, concentrated crops.

## Likely exam angles

**"Explain how the Cape Doctor affects grape growing in South Africa."** Note this
demands *both* the benefit and the drawback — a one-sided answer loses marks.

> "The Cape Doctor is a strong south-easterly wind that blows across the Cape in
> summer. It cools the vineyards, slowing ripening and helping to retain acidity,
> and it dries the grape bunches and canopy, which significantly reduces the risk
> of fungal disease. However, the same wind can damage young shoots and disrupt
> flowering, leading to poor fruit set and reduced yields, so growers may need to
> shelter vineyards or select more protected sites."

**"Explain how South Africa produces cool-climate wine styles despite its warm
Mediterranean climate."**

> "The cold Benguela Current flows north along the coast, cooling the air above it
> so that coastal districts such as Walker Bay and Elim ripen slowly and retain
> high acidity. The south-easterly Cape Doctor further cools vineyards through the
> growing season. In addition, vineyards planted at altitude on mountain foothills,
> as at Elgin, and those on cooler south-facing aspects, experience lower average
> temperatures than the valley floors. Together these allow varieties such as
> Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir to be grown successfully."
