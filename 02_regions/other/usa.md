# USA (California, Oregon, Washington, New York)

## California: the climate mechanism that explains everything
California sits at a latitude that should be far too hot for fine wine, and inland
it is. What rescues it is the **cold California Current** running south down the
coast, and the way that cold air reaches the vineyards.

The mechanism is worth learning as a chain, because it generates exam answers:

1. Inland California heats up strongly during the day.
2. That rising hot air creates an area of **low pressure** inland.
3. Cold, foggy air off the Pacific is **drawn inland to fill it**.
4. The fog and cool air penetrate through **gaps and river valleys in the Coastal
   Ranges** — the Golden Gate, the Petaluma Gap, and the opening onto San Pablo Bay.
5. Vineyards near those gaps are dramatically cooler than sites only a few miles
   away that sit behind unbroken hills.

So proximity to a **gap** matters more than proximity to the ocean in a straight
line. Fog typically burns off by midday, giving cool mornings and warm afternoons.

- Hazards: **drought** and water restriction, **wildfire and smoke taint** (an
  increasingly examinable modern risk), and spring frost in valley floors.

## Napa Valley
Small, warm and heavily reputation-driven. The critical structural facts:

- **It gets warmer as you move north up the valley, away from San Pablo Bay.**
  Los Carneros at the southern mouth is the coolest, cooled by bay fog and wind,
  and grows Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. Moving north through Oakville and
  Rutherford to St Helena and **Calistoga** at the northern end, it becomes
  progressively hotter, and the wines fuller and riper.
- **Mountain vs valley floor** is the other axis, and a favourite exam angle.
  Hillside AVAs such as **Howell Mountain**, **Mount Veeder** and **Spring
  Mountain** sit **above the fog line**. They therefore get *more* sunshine hours
  but *lower* temperatures than the valley floor below, along with poorer,
  thinner, free-draining soils. The result is lower yields and wines with more
  concentration and firmer tannin structure.
- **Stags Leap District** is on the eastern side, catching afternoon sun on its
  rocky hillsides but cooled by evening breezes off the bay, giving Cabernet with
  a characteristically softer, more supple texture.
- Grapes: **Cabernet Sauvignon** above all, plus Merlot, Cabernet Franc, and
  Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc in the cooler south.

## Sonoma County
Larger and more climatically varied than Napa.
- **Russian River Valley** — cooled by fog drawn through the Petaluma Gap; Pinot
  Noir and Chardonnay.
- **Sonoma Coast** — genuinely cool and maritime.
- **Alexander Valley** — warmer and inland; Cabernet Sauvignon.
- **Dry Creek Valley** — warm; the classic home of old-vine **Zinfandel**.
- **Los Carneros** — shared with Napa, cool, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, and an
  important source of base wine for traditional method sparkling.

## Other California
- **Lodi** — inland in the Central Valley and warm, but cooled meaningfully by
  breezes off the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta. A major source of high-volume
  wine, but also of genuinely old **Zinfandel** vines.
- **Monterey**, **Santa Barbara County** and **Santa Maria Valley** — the Salinas
  Valley and the transverse (east–west running) valleys of Santa Barbara funnel
  ocean air far inland, making them unusually cool for their latitude. Pinot Noir
  and Chardonnay.
- **Paso Robles** — warm, with a wide diurnal range; Rhône varieties and Cabernet.
- **Santa Cruz Mountains** — high, cool, low-yielding.

## Zinfandel: the variety with a technical problem
Zinfandel **ripens unevenly within a single bunch**. At any moment a bunch can hold
green berries, perfectly ripe berries and raisined ones at the same time. Growers
must choose when to pick, and picking late enough to avoid green flavours means
accepting some raisining, which drives **high sugar levels and therefore high
alcohol**. This is why Zinfandel is characteristically full-bodied and high in
alcohol, often with a jammy or raisiny note. It is also made as off-dry pink
**White Zinfandel**.

## Oregon — Willamette Valley
Cool and genuinely marginal, sheltered from the worst Pacific weather by the Coast
Range but still exposed to **rain around harvest**, which is the main vintage risk
and the source of real vintage variation. Almost synonymous with **Pinot Noir**,
with Pinot Gris and Chardonnay also planted. Oregon law requires a **higher varietal
minimum (90%)** than the federal 75% for most varieties.

## Washington — Columbia Valley
The opposite of Oregon despite sitting alongside it. The **Cascade Mountains cast a
rain shadow** over eastern Washington, making it effectively a desert: sunny, very
dry, and dependent on **irrigation** from the Columbia River. The climate is
**continental**, with a large diurnal range that preserves acidity, and a real risk
of **damaging winter freeze**. Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Riesling.
**Yakima Valley** is its main sub-region.

## New York — Finger Lakes
Cool continental and severely cold in winter. The **deep glacial lakes moderate
winter temperatures**, releasing stored heat and protecting vines from the worst
freeze damage; without them viticulture would be marginal at best. **Riesling** is
the flagship.

## Law: the AVA system
An **American Viticultural Area** defines **origin only**. Unlike AOC or DOC, it
sets **no rules on permitted grape varieties, yields, or winemaking methods** — a
point worth stating explicitly in any question comparing New World and Old World
labelling law. Key percentage rules:
- **85%** of the grapes must come from a stated AVA.
- **75%** must be the stated variety (Oregon requires 90% for most varieties).
- **95%** must be from a stated vintage where an AVA is named.

## Quality/price factors
Volume wine comes from the hot, flat, irrigated, machine-harvested Central Valley.
Premium wine comes from cool fog-influenced sites (Russian River, Carneros, Santa
Maria) or from low-yielding hillside vineyards above the fog line (Howell Mountain,
Mount Veeder). Napa Cabernet additionally carries a scarcity and reputation premium
that is not purely a function of growing conditions — land cost there is itself a
major component of price.

## Likely exam angles

**"Explain how fog influences vineyard sites in California."**

> "Hot inland areas create low pressure that draws cold, foggy air in off the
> Pacific. This air penetrates through gaps in the Coastal Ranges, such as the
> Petaluma Gap and the opening at San Pablo Bay, cooling nearby vineyards such as
> Russian River Valley and Los Carneros. The fog reduces morning temperatures and
> slows ripening, allowing grapes to retain acidity and develop flavour over a
> longer period, which is why cool-climate varieties such as Pinot Noir and
> Chardonnay succeed at a latitude that would otherwise be too warm for them."

**"Explain why hillside vineyards in Napa produce different wines from valley
floor vineyards."**

> "Hillside vineyards such as Howell Mountain sit above the fog line, so they
> receive more hours of sunshine but experience lower temperatures than the valley
> floor, which is cooled by fog in the morning and heats up strongly by day. Their
> soils are thinner, poorer and free-draining, which restricts vine vigour and
> reduces yields. This means the fruit is more concentrated, producing wines with
> greater intensity and firmer tannin structure than those from the deeper, more
> fertile valley floor soils."
