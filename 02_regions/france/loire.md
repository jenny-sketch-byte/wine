# The Loire Valley

The Loire runs west to east, and its climate changes along that axis. Getting the
sequence right unlocks most Loire questions:

**Nantais** (maritime, on the Atlantic) → **Anjou-Saumur** → **Touraine** →
**Central Vineyards** (fully continental, inland)

So the far west is mild and damp with a real fungal risk, and the far east has
warm summers, cold winters and a genuine spring frost problem.

---

## Nantais — Muscadet
- **Climate:** **Maritime**, right on the Atlantic. Mild, damp, with rainfall
  spread through the year and consequent **fungal disease** pressure. **Spring
  frost is the major hazard** and has caused severe crop losses.
- **Grape:** **Melon Blanc** (Melon de Bourgogne). Neutral, light-bodied, high
  acidity, low aromatics — a grape with very little to say for itself, which is
  precisely why the winemaking matters.
- **Winemaking — *sur lie*:** The examinable technique. The wine is left on its
  **fine lees** (dead yeast cells) after fermentation, in the **same vessel**, and
  must be bottled directly off them. The legal minimum is that it stays on the lees
  until at least the **March following the harvest**.
  - Effect: **autolysis** releases compounds that add **body, a creamy or bready
    texture and savoury complexity** to a wine that would otherwise be thin and
    neutral. It also commonly retains a slight **spritz** of dissolved CO₂, adding
    freshness.
  - The best wines come from **Muscadet Sèvre et Maine**, the principal sub-zone.

## Anjou-Saumur
- **Climate:** Transitional between maritime and continental; warmer and drier than
  Nantais, with some sheltered sites.
- **Chenin Blanc** dominates the whites, and this is the region where its full
  range shows:
  - **Savennières** — dry, structured, high acid, ageworthy.
  - **Coteaux du Layon** — **sweet**, from **botrytis-affected** grapes. The Layon
    river valley generates **autumn morning mists that encourage noble rot**, which
    then burn off in afternoon sunshine — the humidity-then-dryness cycle noble rot
    requires. *Bonnezeaux* and *Quarts de Chaume* are its top sub-appellations.
- **Cabernet Franc** dominates the reds — **Saumur-Champigny** is the key name.
  Light to medium body, high acidity, red fruit, and a leafy or herbaceous note
  from pyrazines when not fully ripe.
- **Rosé:** **Rosé d'Anjou** (from Grolleau, medium-dry) and **Cabernet d'Anjou**
  (from Cabernet Franc/Sauvignon, medium-sweet and higher quality).
- Sparkling **Saumur** is made by the traditional method.

## Touraine — Vouvray and Chinon
- **Climate:** Continental influence strengthening; warm summers, cold winters,
  frost risk.
- **Soils:** **Tuffeau** — a soft, porous limestone. It retains water, helping vines
  through dry summers, and the caves cut into it are used for both ageing and, in
  the past, dwelling.
- **Vouvray** — **Chenin Blanc**, and the classic demonstration of a grape whose
  style is decided by the vintage. In a warm year with botrytis, sweet
  *moelleux* wines are made; in a cool year the fruit goes to dry (*sec*) or
  sparkling wine. Chenin's naturally **very high acidity** is what makes both the
  sweet wines balanced and the dry wines long-lived.
  - Chenin **ripens unevenly**, so growers make **successive passes (*tries*)**
    through the vineyard, picking only the ripest or most botrytised bunches each
    time. This is labour-intensive and a direct cost/price factor.
- **Chinon** and **Bourgueil** — **Cabernet Franc** reds on tuffeau and gravel;
  the gravel sites ripen more fully and give richer, more structured wine.

## Central Vineyards — Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé
- **Climate:** **Continental**, the coolest and most inland part of the Loire. Warm
  summers, cold winters, and a **significant spring frost risk** as budburst can
  precede the last frosts.
- **Grape:** **Sauvignon Blanc**. High acidity, pronounced aromatics — gooseberry,
  grass, blackcurrant leaf, citrus — driven by thiols and pyrazines. Usually
  unoaked to preserve those aromatics, though a minority of producers use old oak.
- **Soils — the examinable detail:**
  - **Kimmeridgian marl (limestone and clay)** — the same formation as Chablis;
    gives structure and body.
  - ***Caillottes*** — shallow, stony limestone; gives lighter, more aromatic wine
    that is ready earlier.
  - ***Silex* (flint)** — associated particularly with **Pouilly-Fumé**, and
    traditionally credited with its smoky, gunflint character.
- **Sancerre** also produces Pinot Noir reds and rosés. **Menetou-Salon** is a
  cheaper neighbouring alternative of similar character.

## Law
AOC, with **Sur lie** as a legally defined term in Muscadet. Loire sweet wines are
labelled by residual sugar terms (*sec*, *demi-sec*, *moelleux*, *doux*).

## Quality/price factors
Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé carry a strong reputation premium, and Menetou-Salon is
the standard "same style, lower price" answer. Sweet Coteaux du Layon and Quarts de
Chaume are expensive because botrytis is unpredictable, yields from shrivelled
berries are tiny, and multiple hand-picking passes are required. Muscadet remains
inexpensive because the grape is neutral, yields are decent and the region has no
comparable reputation premium.

## Likely exam angles

**"Explain how *sur lie* ageing affects the style of Muscadet."**

> "After fermentation the wine is left in contact with its fine lees in the same
> vessel until at least the March following harvest. As the dead yeast cells break
> down through autolysis, they release compounds that give the wine additional
> body, a creamier texture and savoury, bready complexity. The wine also often
> retains a light spritz of carbon dioxide. This matters because Melon Blanc is a
> neutral, light-bodied and high-acid grape, so without lees contact the wine would
> be thin and lacking in flavour."

**"Explain why a range of sweetness levels is produced in Vouvray."**

> "Vouvray is made from Chenin Blanc in a region with a continental climate and
> significant vintage variation. In warm years with autumn mists, noble rot
> develops and grapes reach high sugar levels, allowing sweet moelleux wines to be
> made. In cooler years the grapes do not ripen as fully, so the fruit is used for
> dry or sparkling wine instead. Chenin Blanc's naturally high acidity balances the
> sugar in the sweet wines and gives the dry wines the structure to age."
