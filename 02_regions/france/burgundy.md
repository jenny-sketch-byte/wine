# Burgundy

## Climate
- Type: **Cool continental**
- Key factor: inland, no major moderating water body — cold winters, warm-but-variable summers
- Effects: marginal ripening climate for Pinot Noir/Chardonnay — vintage variation is significant (frost, poor flowering, rain at harvest are all real risks)
- Hazards: spring frost (notably in Chablis, further north and more exposed), hail, uneven flowering (coulure/millerandage) in poor years

## Soils — the core exam theme: terroir & fragmentation
- **Kimmeridgian limestone and marl** dominate the Côte d'Or escarpment — limestone provides drainage and reflects light/heat, marl (clay+limestone) adds water retention
- Extreme **parcel fragmentation**: the same slope has dozens of distinct climats (named vineyard parcels) with subtly different soil/aspect/drainage, each historically mapped by monks — the basis of Burgundy's uniquely detailed vineyard classification
- **Aspect** matters hugely on the narrow Côte d'Or escarpment: east-facing slopes get morning sun, best exposure sits mid-slope (better drainage than valley floor, warmer than the very top)

## Key grapes
- **Pinot Noir** (red) — thin-skinned, early-budding (frost risk), highly terroir-expressive, low tannin/colour relative to Bordeaux reds, prized for aromatic complexity over power
- **Chardonnay** (white) — very adaptable, expresses terroir and winemaking choice (oak, MLF, lees) clearly, ranges from lean/mineral (Chablis) to rich/buttery (Meursault)
- **Gamay** — Beaujolais (administratively part of greater Burgundy, distinct style — see carbonic maceration in `01_foundations/winemaking.md`)
- **Aligoté** — secondary white grape, lighter/higher acid than Chardonnay

## Appellation hierarchy (the quality pyramid — know this cold)
1. **Regional** (e.g. Bourgogne AOC) — broadest, can be sourced from anywhere within Burgundy
2. **Village** (e.g. Gevrey-Chambertin, Meursault) — from a specific commune
3. **Premier Cru** — named, higher-quality vineyard parcels within a village
4. **Grand Cru** — the very top vineyard sites, own appellation (doesn't carry the village name), tiny production, highest prices
- Roughly: ~50% regional, ~35% village, ~10% Premier Cru, ~1–2% Grand Cru of total production — scarcity at the top drives price

## Winemaking notes
- Reds: whole-bunch inclusion is a notable stylistic variable (adds stem tannin, herbal lift) — a live producer choice, not a fixed regional rule
- Whites: barrel fermentation and lees ageing (with/without bâtonnage) are central to style, especially in the Côte de Beaune (Meursault, Puligny/Chassagne-Montrachet)
- Chablis (northern outlier): typically stainless steel or neutral oak, no MLF suppression issue but style leans lean/high-acid/mineral due to cooler climate + Kimmeridgian soil, distinct from riper Côte de Beaune whites

## Styles produced
- Dry red (Pinot Noir) — Côte de Nuits especially
- Dry white (Chardonnay) — Côte de Beaune, Chablis, Côte Chalonnaise, Mâconnais
- Sparkling: Crémant de Bourgogne (traditional method, regional level)

## Quality/price factors
- Vineyard classification tier (Grand Cru >> Premier Cru >> Village >> Regional) is the primary price driver
- Fragmented ownership (many growers hold small parcels within a single climat) means producer name matters enormously even within the same vineyard classification
- Low yields required at Grand Cru/Premier Cru level, combined with tiny total production, sustains high prices

## Likely exam angles
**"Explain how Burgundy's appellation hierarchy reflects differences in vineyard quality."**

Practice answer (template-based):
> "Burgundy's appellation system is based on a hierarchy of vineyard sites, from broad regional appellations up to tightly delimited Grand Cru parcels. This is due to centuries of observation of how soil, drainage, and aspect vary even within a single slope on the Côte d'Or, meaning the narrowest, most historically consistent sites were identified as Grand Cru. This results in wines from higher tiers commanding significantly higher prices, reflecting both the scarcity of the sites and their more consistent quality."
