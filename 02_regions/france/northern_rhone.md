# Northern Rhône

## Climate
- Type: **Moderate continental** — and this is the first thing to get right, because
  the Southern Rhône is **Mediterranean**. Any question comparing the two banks on
  climate hinges on this distinction.
- The north is meaningfully **cooler** than the south, with a shorter growing season
  and more vintage variation. Rainfall falls mainly outside the growing season.
- **The Mistral** is the defining wind: a cold, strong, northerly wind funnelling
  down the Rhône valley. As with the Cape Doctor, it cuts both ways and both halves
  earn marks. It **cools the vineyards and dries the canopy, reducing fungal
  disease**, and it helps preserve acidity. But it can **break shoots, disrupt
  flowering and reduce yields**, which is why vines here are often trained low and
  individually **staked** — the traditional *échalas* system, where two or three
  stakes are tied into a tepee around each vine to hold it against the wind.
- Hazards: Mistral damage, spring frost, and poor flowering in cool, wet springs.

## Site: the reason this region exists
The Northern Rhône is a narrow strip of extremely **steep granite slopes** along the
river. Two things follow:
- Slopes are terraced, and almost all work must be done **by hand** — no
  mechanisation is possible on many sites. This is a direct and frequently examined
  cost/price factor.
- Aspect is everything. The best sites face **south or south-east**, maximising sun
  interception at a latitude and in a climate where full ripening is not guaranteed.
  **Côte Rôtie** translates as "roasted slope" for exactly this reason.

## Soils
Predominantly **granite**, which is free-draining and stores heat. Côte Rôtie
distinguishes **Côte Brune** (darker, iron-rich clay, giving more structured,
tannic, ageworthy wines) from **Côte Blonde** (lighter soils with limestone and
schist, giving softer, more perfumed wines). Hermitage's hill also varies, with
granite at the top and more alluvial soil and clay at the base.

## Key grapes
- **Syrah — the only black grape permitted in the Northern Rhône.** This is a
  reliable MCQ fact and a hard contrast with the Southern Rhône's large permitted
  blend. Northern Rhône Syrah gives medium to deep colour, high tannin, medium to
  high acidity, and flavours of black fruit, black pepper, violet and, with age,
  meat and smoke.
- **Viognier** — white, highly aromatic (apricot, blossom), low acidity, needs to be
  picked in a narrow window: too early and it is neutral, too late and it is flabby
  and heavy in alcohol.
- **Marsanne** and **Roussanne** — the white blending pair for Hermitage,
  Crozes-Hermitage and Saint-Joseph. Marsanne gives weight and body; Roussanne gives
  aroma and acidity but is harder to grow.

## The appellations
| Appellation | Wine | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Côte Rôtie** | Red only | Syrah with **up to 20% Viognier permitted, co-fermented**. Steep terraces, Brune/Blonde divide. |
| **Condrieu** | White only | **100% Viognier**. Aromatic, full-bodied, low acid, usually dry, rarely oaked heavily. |
| **Château-Grillet** | White only | A tiny single-estate appellation within Condrieu; Viognier. |
| **Hermitage** | Red & white | A single south-facing hill. Syrah, with **up to 15% Marsanne/Roussanne permitted**. Powerful, tannic, very long-lived. |
| **Crozes-Hermitage** | Red & white | The largest appellation, on flatter land surrounding the Hermitage hill. More variable, better value, generally lighter. |
| **Saint-Joseph** | Red & white | A long, narrow strip on the west bank; lighter and more fruit-forward than Hermitage. |
| **Cornas** | **Red only** | 100% Syrah — **no white grapes permitted at all**. A sheltered sun-trap producing deeply coloured, powerful, tannic wine. |
| **Saint-Péray** | White only | Still and traditional method sparkling, from Marsanne and Roussanne. |

## Winemaking notes
- **Co-fermentation of Viognier with Syrah in Côte Rôtie** is the most examinable
  technique. Fermenting the white grapes together with the black ones **stabilises
  colour** through co-pigmentation and adds an **aromatic floral lift** to the
  finished red. Note the permitted maximum is 20%, but in practice most producers
  use far less or none at all.
- **Whole bunch fermentation** is traditional and still widespread, adding aromatic
  complexity and a fresher, more perfumed character.
- Maturation traditionally in **large old oak** (*foudres*), which allows slow
  oxygenation and tannin softening without adding obvious new-oak flavour; some
  modern producers use new barriques for a more overtly oaky style.

## Quality/price factors
Prices in the Northern Rhône are driven above all by **scarcity and labour**. The
appellations are tiny, the slopes are too steep to mechanise, terraces need constant
maintenance, and yields are low. Hermitage and Côte Rôtie command the highest prices;
Crozes-Hermitage, sitting on flat, mechanisable land, is markedly cheaper and is the
standard example of how site topography feeds directly into price.

## Likely exam angles

**"Explain how the Mistral affects grape growing in the Northern Rhône."** Two-sided
answer required.

> "The Mistral is a strong, cold northerly wind that blows down the Rhône valley. It
> cools the vineyards, helping the grapes retain acidity, and it dries the canopy
> and bunches after rain, significantly reducing the risk of fungal disease. However
> it is strong enough to break young shoots and disrupt flowering, which reduces
> yields, so growers train vines low and stake them individually to protect them."

**"Explain why Northern Rhône Syrah is expensive relative to Southern Rhône reds."**

> "Northern Rhône vineyards are planted on very steep granite slopes that must be
> terraced, which means all vineyard work and harvesting has to be carried out by
> hand rather than by machine, greatly increasing labour costs. The appellations are
> also very small and yields are low, so supply is limited. In contrast, much of the
> Southern Rhône is flatter land that can be worked mechanically at higher yields,
> so its wines can be produced far more cheaply."
