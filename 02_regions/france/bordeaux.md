# Bordeaux

## Climate
- Type: **Maritime** (moderate)
- Key factor: Atlantic Ocean + Gironde estuary and its tributaries (Dordogne, Garonne) moderate temperature, keep frost risk down in spring
- Effects: long, relatively mild growing season; vintage variation is significant: rain during flowering (poor fruit set) or at harvest (dilution/rot) is the main risk
- Hazards: spring frost, rain at harvest, occasional hail

## Soils: the Left Bank / Right Bank divide (core exam theme)
| Bank | Soil | Dominant grape | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Left Bank** (Médoc, Graves) | Gravel, well-drained, poor fertility, retains daytime heat | **Cabernet Sauvignon**-dominant blends | Gravel drains well (suits Cabernet's later ripening, vigour needs control) and reflects/retains heat, aiding ripening |
| **Right Bank** (Saint-Émilion, Pomerol) | Clay and limestone, cooler, more water-retentive | **Merlot**-dominant (with Cabernet Franc) | Clay retains moisture/cool: suits Merlot which buds/ripens earlier and is more sensitive to drought stress |

## Key grapes
- **Cabernet Sauvignon**: thick skin, high tannin, late-ripening, needs warm, well-drained sites
- **Merlot**: earlier-ripening, softer tannin, more approachable young, prone to uneven ripening in cool/wet years
- **Cabernet Franc**: earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon, aromatic, adds lift/perfume (key in Right Bank blends, especially Cheval Blanc style)
- **Petit Verdot / Malbec**: minor blending components, add colour/spice
- White: **Sémillon** (body, susceptible to noble rot) + **Sauvignon Blanc** (acidity, aromatics) blended for both dry whites and sweet (Sauternes/Barsac)

## Appellation hierarchy
- Regional: **Bordeaux AOC** / **Bordeaux Supérieur** (broadest, any location within Bordeaux)
- Sub-regional: **Médoc**, **Graves**, etc.
- Communal/village: **Margaux, Pauillac, Saint-Julien, Saint-Estèphe** (Left Bank); **Saint-Émilion, Pomerol** (Right Bank): tighter geographic and quality rules
- **Classification systems** (separate from AOC, producer-based not appellation-based):
  - **1855 Classification** (Left Bank, Sauternes): fixed list of Crus Classés, rarely revised
  - **Saint-Émilion Classification**: revised periodically (Premier Grand Cru Classé A/B, Grand Cru Classé)
  - Pomerol has **no official classification**: reputation/price alone signals quality tier

## Winemaking notes
- Blending is the norm (rarely single-varietal): allows balancing across vintage variation (e.g. more Merlot in a cool year for ripeness insurance)
- New French oak barrique widely used for premium reds: adds structure/ageing potential
- Sauternes/Barsac: noble rot (Botrytis) driven by morning mist off the Ciron river + afternoon sun: concentrates Sémillon/Sauvignon Blanc for sweet wine

## Styles produced
- Dry red (majority, by far the most important commercially)
- Dry white (Graves, entry-level Bordeaux Blanc)
- Sweet white (Sauternes, Barsac: botrytized)

## Quality/price factors
- Classification tier (Cru Classé vs Cru Bourgeois vs generic Bordeaux AOC)
- Vintage variation is a major price driver: "good vintage" years command significant premiums due to weather-driven quality difference
- Single-estate (château) branding and reputation drive much of the top-end pricing beyond soil/appellation alone

## Likely exam angles
**"Explain why Cabernet Sauvignon dominates the Left Bank while Merlot dominates the Right Bank."**

Practice answer (template-based):
> "The Left Bank has well-drained gravel soils that retain daytime heat, suiting the late-ripening, vigorous Cabernet Sauvignon, which needs a long warm season to fully ripen its thick skins. The Right Bank has cooler, water-retentive clay and limestone soils, which suit the earlier-ripening Merlot that is more prone to drought stress. This means the two banks favour different grapes based on how well each variety's ripening needs match the drainage and heat-retention properties of the soil."
