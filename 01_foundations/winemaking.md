# Winemaking: Foundations

## White winemaking
1. **Harvest & sorting** → whole-bunch or destemmed.
2. **Pressing**: usually pressed before fermentation (skins removed early) to avoid tannin/colour extraction. Gentle pneumatic press preferred for quality: reduces harsh phenolics.
3. **Skin contact (optional)**: brief pre-fermentation maceration (hours) to extract aroma/flavour compounds from skins: used selectively (e.g. some Sauvignon Blanc, Gewurztraminer) since too long risks bitterness/phenolic harshness.
4. **Settling/clarification** of juice before fermentation (reduces solids → cleaner ferment).
5. **Fermentation**: often at cooler temperatures (12–18°C) in stainless steel to preserve fresh fruit aromatics; barrel fermentation (warmer, often in oak) builds texture/complexity for fuller styles (e.g. white Burgundy).
6. **Malolactic conversion (MLC/MLF)**: optional: bacteria convert sharp malic acid → softer lactic acid + diacetyl (buttery character). Used for fuller/rounder styles (Chardonnay), avoided for aromatic/high-acid styles (Riesling, most Sauvignon Blanc).
7. **Lees ageing (sur lie)**, optional: ageing on dead yeast cells post-fermentation, with or without stirring (bâtonnage). Adds texture, richness, some autolytic character (bready notes with extended contact, as in Champagne/Muscadet sur lie).

## Rosé methods (know all three)
| Method | Process | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct pressing** | Red grapes pressed immediately, brief skin contact during pressing only | Palest, most delicate rosé (Provence style) |
| **Short maceration** | Red grapes macerate on skins briefly (hours) before pressing off as if for white wine | Light-medium colour, more fruit character |
| **Saignée ("bleeding")** | Juice "bled off" early from a red wine fermentation, concentrating the remaining red wine and producing rosé as a byproduct | Often deeper colour/more structure; sometimes seen as a secondary product of red wine production |

## Red winemaking
1. **Destemming** (usually): stems can add harsh, green tannin if included (whole-bunch/whole-cluster is a deliberate stylistic choice in some regions, e.g. some Burgundy, some Beaujolais/carbonic).
2. **Crushing** → must (juice + skins + seeds) ferments together: this is the core difference from white winemaking: colour, tannin, and flavour all extracted from skins during fermentation.
3. **Cap management**: skins/solids float to form a "cap" during fermentation: must be mixed back in for extraction:
   - **Punch-down (pigeage)**: pushing the cap down into the juice: gentler extraction.
   - **Pump-over (remontage)**: pumping juice from the bottom over the cap: more aeration, can be more assertive extraction.
4. **Maceration**: time in contact with skins.
   - Extended maceration (beyond fermentation) = more tannin/colour/structure extraction, used for age-worthy, full-bodied styles.
5. **Malolactic conversion**: near-universal for red wine: softens acidity, adds stability, generally considered essential for quality reds.
6. **Pressing**: free-run juice (higher quality, released without pressure) vs press wine (from pressing remaining skins, more tannic/harsher): press wine may be blended back in varying proportions depending on style.

## Carbonic & semi-carbonic maceration
- Whole uncrushed bunches placed in a CO2-rich, sealed environment → intracellular fermentation occurs inside intact berries.
- Produces low-tannin, fruity, often bubblegum/banana-note wines for early drinking: signature of Beaujolais Nouveau/Beaujolais generally.
- Semi-carbonic: some berries crush naturally under their own weight, conventional yeast fermentation happens alongside the carbonic effect.

## Sweet wine methods (overview: full detail lives in fortified/regional files)
- **Late harvest**: extended hang time concentrates sugar naturally.
- **Noble rot (botrytis)**: shrivels grapes, concentrating sugar/acid/flavour (Sauternes, Alsace SGN, Tokaji).
- **Fortification**: adding neutral spirit to stop fermentation, retaining residual sugar (see `04_fortified/`).
- **Chaptalization**: adding sugar to the must before/during fermentation to raise potential alcohol: legal in some cool climates for correcting under-ripeness, NOT a sweetening technique (sugar is fully fermented to alcohol).
- **Acidification**: adding acid (e.g. tartaric acid) in warm climates where natural acidity is too low.

## Fermentation vessel choice (recurring theme across regions)
| Vessel | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Stainless steel** | Neutral, preserves fresh fruit character and acidity, precise temperature control |
| **Concrete (incl. eggs)** | Neutral-ish, some texture benefit from gentle lees movement, temperature buffering |
| **Oak (barrel/foudre)** | Adds flavour compounds, allows micro-oxygenation, textural roundness (see `01_foundations/oak.md`) |

## Likely exam angles
**"Explain how maceration/cap management techniques affect the style of a red wine."**

Practice answer (template-based):
> "Extended maceration keeps the must in contact with grape skins for longer after fermentation, which extracts more tannin and colour compounds. This results in a wine with greater structure and ageing potential, suited to premium, age-worthy styles rather than wines intended for early drinking."
