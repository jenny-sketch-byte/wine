# Oak — Foundations

## Why oak is used (three separate effects — don't conflate them)
1. **Flavour**: oak-derived compounds add aroma/flavour (vanilla, toast, spice, coconut, smoke).
2. **Tannin/structure**: oak (especially new) contributes tannin, adding structure/texture and ageing potential.
3. **Micro-oxygenation**: barrels are not airtight — slow oxygen ingress softens tannins over time and stabilizes colour (in reds), and can add complexity/nutty/oxidative notes (whites).

## New vs old oak
- **New oak**: strongest flavour and tannin contribution — vanilla, toast, spice, structure.
- **Old/neutral oak**: minimal flavour impact (compounds already leached out by prior fills), used mainly for the micro-oxygenation/texture effect without adding new oak flavour — lets fruit character show through.
- Producers often blend a percentage of new oak (e.g. "30% new") to balance oak influence with fruit character and cost (new barrels are expensive).

## Barrel size (bigger = less relative oak influence)
| Vessel | Typical size | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Barrique** | ~225L | High surface-area-to-volume ratio → strong oak flavour/tannin influence relative to wine volume |
| **Puncheon/hogshead** | ~300–500L | Moderate influence |
| **Foudre (large oak vat)** | 1,000L+ | Low relative oak flavour, still allows micro-oxygenation — common where texture/ageing is wanted without heavy oak flavour (e.g. some Rhône, Alsace) |

## Origin: French vs American oak (classic exam contrast)
| | French oak | American oak |
|---|---|---|
| Grain | Tight | Looser/wider |
| Flavour profile | Subtle spice, cedar, fine tannin | Bolder vanilla, coconut, dill |
| Cost | More expensive (tighter grain requires splitting, not sawing) | Cheaper |
| Typical use | Bordeaux, Burgundy, many premium reds/whites globally | Rioja (traditional), some New World Cabernet/Shiraz |

## Toast level
- Barrels are "toasted" (charred internally) during coopering — light/medium/heavy toast.
- Higher toast = more toasty/smoky/caramel character, can also soften perceived tannin from the wood itself.

## Oak alternatives (cost-driven, entry-level wines)
- **Oak chips/staves/powder** added to inert vessels (steel/concrete): cheap way to add oak flavour compounds without barrel cost, but no micro-oxygenation benefit — flavour without the structural/ageing effect.
- A key quality/price marker: real barrel ageing (labour, barrel cost, cellar space, evaporation/"angel's share" losses) vs alternatives signals a real cost difference reflected in price tier.

## Oak & style — where it's used vs avoided
- **Used**: full-bodied whites (Chardonnay), structured reds (Bordeaux, Rioja Gran Reserva, Barolo — often large old oak/botti), some fortified wines (Sherry solera, tawny Port).
- **Avoided/minimal**: aromatic whites where freshness/varietal aromatics are the point (Riesling, most Sauvignon Blanc, Muscat) — oak would mask delicate aromatics.

## Likely exam angles
**"Explain how the choice of oak vessel affects the style of a wine."**

Practice answer (template-based):
> "Ageing in a new, small barrique gives a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, extracting pronounced vanilla, toast, and tannin from the wood, while allowing slow oxygen ingress that softens the wine's structure over time. This results in a wine with greater richness, spice character, and ageing potential compared to one aged in neutral, large-format oak or stainless steel."
